# Intertextual Sources, Mythological Frameworks, and Cosmological Systems in *Finnegans Wake*

James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake* is an encyclopedic dream-narrative, dense with allusions, mythology, and multi-layered puns. Joyce constructs a universal history and cosmology by synthesizing diverse cultural frameworks into a cyclical narrative of fall, death, and resurrection.

---

## 1. Egyptian Book of the Dead & The Osiris Myth

Joyce heavily utilized the *Egyptian Book of the Dead* (specifically E.A. Wallis Budge's 1895 translation of the *Papyrus of Ani*, [available at Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1300)) as a structural and thematic model for the *Wake*.

### John Bishop's *Joyce's Book of the Dark* (1986)

Bishop's influential thesis argues that the *Wake* is a profound reconstruction of the nocturnal, sleeping experience. He suggests the *Egyptian Book of the Dead* (literally translated as *Chapters of Coming Forth by Day*) provided Joyce with the perfect mythological template to chart the dreamer's descent into the unconscious "underworld" of sleep, equating sleep with temporary death and morning with resurrection. Joyce even referred to his own work-in-progress as "Joyce's Book of the Dead" to his friend Frank Budgen.

### The Osiris Myth in the *Wake*

- **HCE as Osiris:** Humphrey Chimpden Earwicker (HCE) embodies Osiris, the slain and dismembered king of the underworld. Like Osiris, HCE is "fallen"—publicly tried, scattered across the landscape, and awaiting resurrection.
- **ALP as Isis:** Anna Livia Plurabelle (ALP) is cast as Isis, the restorative feminine principle, collecting the fragments of her fallen husband to piece him back together for resurrection. Her river flow = the annual Nile flood that fertilizes Egypt.
- **The Weighing of the Heart:** The Hall of Ma'at (where the deceased's heart is weighed against the feather of truth) parallels HCE's trials and judgment throughout the *Wake*, particularly in Books I and III.
- **Book IV / The Ricorso:** The final chapter of the *Wake*, breaking into the dawn, mirrors the Egyptian narrative of resurrection and the soul "coming forth by day." It initiates the cyclical return to the opening of the book, echoing the solar cycle central to Egyptian cosmology.

**Key Source:** Budge, E.A. Wallis, trans. *The Book of the Dead: The Papyrus of Ani*. London: British Museum, 1895. [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1300)

## 2. The Book of Kells & Irish Epigraphy

Joyce saw a structural analogy between his own densely illuminated text and the labyrinthine Celtic knotwork of medieval Irish manuscripts.

### The Tunc Page (TCD MS 58)

The *Book of Kells*, housed in Trinity College Dublin, heavily influenced Joyce, particularly the "Tunc" page (folio 124r, illustrating Matthew 27:38: *"Tunc crucifixerant XPI cum eo duos latrones"* / "Then were there two thieves crucified with him"). Joyce famously boasted of having provided the "key" to its labyrinth in the *Wake*.

The Tunc page directly models the complex, maze-like nature of *Wake*'s Chapter I.5 (pp. 104–125, the "Mamafesta" chapter), where ALP's letter is analyzed as an artifact like a manuscript.

### The Hen and the Letter

In the "Mamafesta" chapter, a hen (Biddy Doran) scratches a mysterious letter from a dung-heap. This act of unearthing allegorizes:
1. The Irish monks illuminating the *Book of Kells* by lamplight.
2. The later rediscovery of the *Book of Kells* (stolen in 1007, recovered from the ground in 1007 AD).
3. The parallel between the hen's scratches in the earth and the intricate Celtic interlace on vellum.

**Key Source:** Sullivan, Sir Edward. *The Book of Kells: Reproduced from the Manuscript in Trinity College, Dublin*. London and New York: The Studio, 1914. Joyce possessed a copy of this lavishly illustrated volume.

## 3. Arthurian Legend & Wagner's *Tristan und Isolde*

The romance of Tristan and Isolde serves as a primary vehicle for exploring themes of betrayal, forbidden love, and intergenerational conflict.

### Richard Wagner & *Tristan und Isolde* (1865)

Wagner's opera, with its radical chromatic harmony and concept of the *Liebestod* (love-death), provided the dramatic and musical atmosphere for the *Wake*. As Matthew J.C. Hodgart and Mabel P. Worthington note in *Song in the Works of James Joyce* (Temple University Press, 1959), operatic motifs pervade the text.

### Joseph Bédier's Influence

While Wagner provided the mood, Joyce's textual source was Joseph Bédier's 1900 prose reconstruction, *Le Roman de Tristan et Iseut*. Joyce adopted a "worst-text" method, corrupting and fragmenting Bédier's philological attempt at an "authentic" unified myth.

### The Mark Triangle in Dublin

The Arthurian triangle is transposed onto HCE's family:
- **King Mark:** HCE, the aging king/uncle figure.
- **Isolde (Iseult):** ALP and Issy (the younger, dual personality).
- **Tristram:** Shaun or Shem as the young rival lover.

### Opening Lines

The novel opens with this very motif:
> *"Sir Tristram, violer d'amores, fr'over the short sea, had passencore rearrived from North Armorica on this side the scraggy isthmus of Europe Minor..."* (FW 3.04-07)

This links the Cornish knight directly to the geography of Dublin Bay and **Chapelizod** (from *Chapelle d'Iseult* — the Chapel of Iseult), the village on the Liffey where HCE's pub is located.

**Key Sources:**
- Hodgart, Matthew J.C., and Mabel P. Worthington. *Song in the Works of James Joyce*. Philadelphia: Temple University Press, 1959.
- Bédier, Joseph. *Le Roman de Tristan et Iseut*. Paris: L'Édition d'Art H. Piazza, 1900.

## 4. Jonathan Swift in *Finnegans Wake*

The life and works of Jonathan Swift (1667–1745), Dean of St. Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin, haunt the landscape of the *Wake*, serving as a mirror for aging, mentorship, and split identities.

### Stella and Vanessa

Swift's complex relationships with Esther Johnson (Stella) and Esther Vanhomrigh (Vanessa) are mapped onto Issy's split personality. Joyce frequently references them as the "sosie sesthers" (twin sisters) or notes *"all's fair in vanessy."*

- **Stella:** Esther Johnson, Swift's lifelong companion and possible secret wife. Represents the pure, mature feminine ideal.
- **Vanessa:** Esther Vanhomrigh, Swift's younger pupil and passionate admirer. Represents the dangerous, passionate feminine.

### *Cadenus and Vanessa* (1726)

Swift's autobiographical poem about his fraught romance with his younger pupil plays heavily into the novel, with HCE standing in for *Cadenus* (an anagram for *Decanus*, Dean).

### Intertexts & Language Play

- Both *Gulliver's Travels* and *A Tale of a Tub* provide intertexts for the *Wake*'s satirical dimensions.
- The infantile, playful "little language" Swift used in his *Journal to Stella* acts as a direct predecessor to the polyglot dream-language of the *Wake*.
- Swift is a spectral presence especially in Book IV, representing the troubled, patriarchal intellect approaching death and madness.

## 5. Henrik Ibsen's Master Builder

Joyce was a lifelong champion of the Norwegian playwright Henrik Ibsen (1828–1906), having learned Dano-Norwegian in his youth simply to read Ibsen in the original.

- **Joyce's 1900 Essay:** Joyce published "Ibsen's New Drama" in *The Fortnightly Review* (April 1900), a glowing appreciation of *When We Dead Awaken*. Ibsen himself responded positively through his publisher William Archer in March 1901.
- ***Bygmester Solness* (1892):** Ibsen's play *The Master Builder* features Halvard Solness, an aging architect who climbs a tower and falls to his death, fearing being usurped by the younger generation.
- **Bygmester Finnegan:** Joyce fuses Solness with Tim Finnegan to create "Bygmester Finnegan," the archetypal builder of civilizations. The word functions as a pun on "Big Master," representing HCE. The dizzying ascent and subsequent fall from the scaffold/steeple become central to the *Wake*'s cosmic myth of hubris, collapse, and intergenerational replacement.

## 6. Celtic Mythology & Irish Legend

Irish legend grounds the macro-structure of the *Wake* in a national, pre-Christian landscape.

### Finn MacCool (Fionn Mac Cumhaill)

The mythical warrior-king is the prototype for HCE. The title *Finnegans Wake* itself contains the pun "Finn Again," signaling the return of the ancient hero. The mythology of the Fianna pervades Book I.

### The Sleeping Giant Motif

The myth that Finn MacCool is merely sleeping beneath the Dublin hills, waiting to rise in Ireland's hour of need, is mapped onto the topography: his head at Howth Head, his feet at Phoenix Park, his body the mountain range visible from the bay.

### Historical Mythology

- **Brian Boru at the Battle of Clontarf (1014 AD):** The great high king's victory against the Norse Leinster alliance represents HCE's ambivalent triumph/defeat.
- **The Tuatha Dé Danann:** The mythological cycle of ancient Irish gods populates the dream-history.
- **The Lia Fáil (Stone of Destiny):** Associated with HCE as Stone (in contrast to Shem as Elm).

### Sources

- Gregory, Lady. *Gods and Fighting Men: The Story of the Tuatha De Danaan and of the Fianna of Ireland*. London: John Murray, 1904.
- O'Grady, Standish. *Silva Gadelica: A Collection of Tales in Irish*. London: Williams and Norgate, 1892.

## 7. The Humpty Dumpty Fall Motif & the *Felix Culpa*

The concept of the "Fall" is the foundational comic and cosmic event of the novel, reflecting the theological concept of *Felix Culpa* (the Fortunate Fall — the happy fault that made redemption necessary and possible).

| Type of Fall | Figure / Event |
|:---|:---|
| **Nursery Rhyme** | Humpty Dumpty from the wall (H.C.E. = Humpty Chimpden Earwicker) |
| **Biblical** | Adam's Fall in Eden; Noah's drunkenness (Genesis 3 and 9) |
| **Political** | Charles Stewart Parnell's disgrace and fall (1890–1891) |
| **Folkloric** | Tim Finnegan's fall from the hod-carrier's ladder (from the ballad) |
| **Literary** | Halvard Solness falling from the church tower (*The Master Builder*) |
| **Cosmological** | The Viconian Fall: The divine thunderclap that frightens primitive humans into caves, initiating civilization and language |

Joyce famously encapsulates this universal fall in ten 100-letter "thunderwords." The first is:
> *"The fall (bababadalgharaghtakamminarronnkonnbronntonnerronntuonnthunntrovarrhounawnskawntoohoohoordenenthurnuk!) of a once wallstrait oldparr is retaled early in bed and later on life down through all christian minstrelsy."* (FW 3.15-17)

## 8. Dublin Topography as Universal Landscape

In the *Wake*, the physical map of Dublin becomes the anatomy of the sleeping giant and the stage for universal history.

| Location | FW Role | Universal Analogue |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Howth Head** | Giant's head / HCE's profile | Mount Olympus, Mount Sinai, Great Pyramid |
| **Phoenix Park** | Giant's body / HCE's guilt | Garden of Eden, Elysian Fields, Waterloo battlefield |
| **River Liffey** | ALP's body / the flow of time | Nile, Euphrates, Tiber, Ganges |
| **Chapelizod** | HCE's pub / the world's inn | Universal crossroads, the "Tavern of the World" |
| **Wellington Monument** | Phallic/patriarchal history | Obelisks of ancient Egypt, the Museyroom episode (I.1) |
| **Twelve Pins (Connemara)** | The Twelve Customers / the jury | Twelve apostles, zodiac |
| **The Liffey tributaries** | ALP's 1,000 stream-names | All the rivers of the world |
