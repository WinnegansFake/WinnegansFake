# Multilingualism, Musicology, Textual Editing, and Modern Intertexts

This research document covers several critical scholarly domains of *Finnegans Wake* studies that complement the philosophical and mythological foundations: Joyce's polyglot linguistic architecture, the musicality and street ballad traditions, the 20th-century textual editing wars, and the intersection between the *Wake* and modern physics/linguistics.

---

## 1. The Polyglot Linguistic System ("Wakese")

Joyce constructed *Finnegans Wake* as an artificial, multidimensional dream-language containing vocabulary, syntax, and morphology from approximately **80 different languages and dialects**.

### Key Linguistic Characteristics
- **Portmanteau Formation:** Blending multiple words, etymologies, and semantic registers into single morphological units (e.g., *chaosmos* = chaos + cosmos; *laughtears* = laughter + tears; *funferall* = funeral + fun-for-all).
- **Paronomasia and Phonetic Drift:** Joyce prioritized acoustic effect over orthographic convention. Words frequently evoke multiple languages simultaneously when spoken aloud (e.g., German, Russian, Dutch, Gaelic, Italian, and Latin puns coexisting in a single clause).
- **Overdetermined Signifiers:** In Derek Attridge's *Peculiar Language: Literature as Difference from the Renaissance to James Joyce* (1988), Attridge argues that the *Wake* systematically dismantles the boundaries between the literary and non-literary by exposing the radical instability of language and signifiers.

### Cataloguing the "Small" Languages
Between 1962 and 1980, Clive Hart and Fritz Senn published ***A Wake Newslitter*** (AWN), an academic clearinghouse wherein scholars systematically identified and glossed vocabulary lists of specific languages embedded across the *Wake*:
- **Dounia Bunis Christiani:** *Scandinavian Elements of Finnegans Wake* (1965) — the definitive catalogue of Danish, Norwegian, Swedish, and Old Norse terms.
- **Jack P. Dalton:** "Kiswahili Words in *Finnegans Wake*" (published in Hart & Senn's *A Wake Digest*, 1968).
- **M.J.C. Hodgart:** "Artificial Languages" in the *Wake* (including Esperanto, Volapük, and Novial).
- **Adaline Glasheen:** Studies on Shelta / the secret "cant" and tinker languages of Ireland.
- **C. George Săndulescu:** *A Lexicon of "Small" Languages in Finnegans Wake* (Contemporary Literature Press, 2012) — synthesizing decades of studies covering Basque, Albanian, Armenian, Breton, Finnish, Hebrew, Hungarian, and Romani.

## 2. Musicology, Balladry, and Popular Song

Music is not merely decorative in *Finnegans Wake*; it serves as an acoustic skeleton for the entire narrative.

### Hodgart & Worthington: *Song in the Works of James Joyce* (1959)
Matthew J.C. Hodgart and Mabel P. Worthington documented over **1,000 specific musical allusions** in the *Wake*:
- **Folk Ballads & Music Hall:** Joyce drew extensively from 19th-century Irish street ballads, Dublin music hall routines, and minstrel shows.
- **"The Ballad of Persse O'Reilly" (FW 44–47):** A primary set-piece of the *Wake*, embodying the community's scurrilous, satirical revolt against HCE. In Irish tradition, the *rann* (satirical verse) was believed to possess the magical power to blister a tyrant's skin.
- **Nursery Rhymes as Archetypes:** Simple rhymes like *Humpty Dumpty*, *London Bridge Is Falling Down*, and *Who Killed Cock Robin?* are used repeatedly as universal mythic templates for the Fall, destruction, and resurrection.
- **Operatic Scaffolding:** Beyond folk songs, Wagnerian leitmotif techniques (*Tristan und Isolde*, *Der Ring des Nibelungen*), Thomas Moore's *Irish Melodies*, and Italian opera (Verdi, Bellini, Rossini) provide recurring emotional cadences.

## 3. Textual Editing, Corrigenda, and the Rose/O'Hanlon Controversy

Because Joyce was nearly blind during the composition and proofreading of the 1939 first edition, the integrity of the printed text has generated decades of fierce debate.

### The Textual Lineage
1. **1939 First Edition (Faber / Viking):** Printed with thousands of typographical errors, compositor slips, and missed proof corrections due to Joyce's ocular condition.
2. **1945 Corrigenda:** Joyce and his assistants compiled a list of errata, subsequently incorporated into later printings.
3. **1958 Revised Viking Edition:** Incorporated the corrigenda and established the standard pagination and line count preserved by McHugh's *Annotations*.
4. **The 2010 Danis Rose & John O'Hanlon "Restored" Edition (Houyhnhnm Press / Penguin 2012):**
   - Rose and O'Hanlon spent 30 years collating Joyce's notebooks and draft manuscripts, proposing over **9,000 textual amendments** (adjusting punctuation, spelling, hyphenation, and word order).
   - **The Controversy:** The edition ignited severe disputes in Joycean scholarship. Critics (such as Tim Conley) argued that the editorial rationale lacked transparency, noting that altering word forms in an intentionally unstable, multilingual text risks erasing deliberate Joycean ambiguities and neologisms.

## 4. Modern Science and Physics: Joyce and the "Quark"

The *Wake* famously intersected with 20th-century quantum physics through theoretical physicist **Murray Gell-Mann**.

### The Etymology of the Quark
- In 1963, Gell-Mann proposed the fundamental constituent particles of hadrons. He initially conceived the phonetic sound "kwork".
- Browsing *Finnegans Wake* (Book II, Chapter 4, page 383), he encountered the mocking seabird cry directed at King Mark:
  > *"Three quarks for Muster Mark!*  
  > *Sure he hasn't got much of a bark*  
  > *And sure any he has it's all beside the mark."* (FW 383.01–03)
- Because Gell-Mann's particle model required three constituent entities to form baryons (such as protons and neutrons), the phrase "Three quarks" was an uncanny match. Gell-Mann adopted the spelling "quark," forever linking modern quantum chromodynamics with Joycean dream-language.
