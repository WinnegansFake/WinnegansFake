# Viconian and Brunonian Architectures in *Finnegans Wake*

This research report summarizes the structural and philosophical influences of Giambattista Vico and Giordano Bruno on James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*. It is designed to inform textual analysis, metadata tagging, and the generation of structural SVG visualizations.

---

## 1. Giambattista Vico and *The New Science*

Giambattista Vico (1668–1744) published his mature philosophical work, *Scienza Nuova* (*The New Science*), in three primary editions (1725, 1730, and the definitive 1744 edition). Vico offered a cyclical philosophy of history, opposing Cartesian rationalism.

### Core Concepts

- **The Three Ages of History:** Vico theorized that all nations develop cyclically through three ages:
  1. **Age of Gods (Theocratic):** A time of myth, poetic wisdom, and divine rule. Primitive humans interpret nature as divine (e.g., thunder as the voice of Jove).
  2. **Age of Heroes (Aristocratic):** Defined by aristocratic patricians, hero-worship, and patriarchal slavery (e.g., the era of Achilles and Homer).
  3. **Age of Men (Democratic/Human):** An age of reason, prose, civil equality, and philosophy.
- **The Ricorso:** After the Age of Men decays into a "barbarism of reflection" (civilized corruption and excessive rationality), society collapses and undergoes a *ricorso* (recurrence/reflux), beginning the cycle anew.
- **Storia Ideale Eterna:** The "ideal eternal history," the underlying archetypal pattern that governs the rise and fall of all civilizations in a perpetual *corso-ricorso* rhythm.
- **The Verum-Factum Principle:** "The true is precisely what is made" (*verum esse ipsum factum*). Vico argued we can truly know history because humans made it (unlike nature, which only God made and can therefore truly understand).
- **The Thunderclap and the Three Institutions:** According to Vico, primitive post-diluvian humans wandering the earth were frightened by the first terrifying thunderclaps of Jove. This terror drove them into caves, ending their feral wandering. This sparked the foundation of civilization through three universal human institutions:
  1. **Religion:** Originating from the fear of the divine thunder.
  2. **Marriage:** Establishing fixed families and lineages.
  3. **Burial:** Honoring the dead and laying claim to the earth.

**Standard Citation:** Vico, Giambattista. *The New Science of Giambattista Vico*. Translated by Thomas Goddard Bergin and Max Harold Fisch. Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1948 (revised 1984). (Typically cited by paragraph number).

## 2. Giordano Bruno and the *Coincidentia Oppositorum*

Giordano Bruno of Nola (1548–1600), an Italian philosopher and Dominican friar burnt at the stake for heresy, profoundly influenced Joyce, who studied him closely in Trieste (circa 1903–1904).

- ***De la Causa, Principio et Uno* (1584):** Bruno posits an infinite universe where God is immanent in all things.
- ***Coincidentia Oppositorum* (Coincidence of Opposites):** Bruno's central dialectic asserts that in the infinite, all opposites (light/dark, youth/age, male/female, good/evil) resolve into unity. Extreme limits meet and become identical.
- **Application in *Finnegans Wake*:** Joyce mapped Bruno's dialectic onto the warring twin brothers, **Shem the Penman** (the introverted, exiled artist) and **Shaun the Postman** (the extroverted, conformist man of action). They engage in eternal sibling rivalry, but frequently swap identities, merge into "Shemshaun," and are ultimately revealed as two halves of a single cosmic principle.
- **"The Nolan":** Joyce constantly references Bruno by punning on his birthplace, Nola, merging him with the famous Dublin bookselling firm "Browne and Nolan" (e.g., FW 428.16).
- **Scholarship:** Notable scholars on Joyce's Brunonian dialectic include David Hayman (*The "Wake" in Transit*) and Frances Motz Boldereff, author of *Hermes to His Son Thoth: Being Joyce's Use of Giordano Bruno in Finnegans Wake* (1968).

## 3. Joyce's Direct Use of Vico in *Finnegans Wake*

Joyce adopted Vico's cyclical *storia ideale eterna* not as a rigid dogma, but as a structural scaffolding ("a trellis," as he called it) for the Wake.

### Samuel Beckett's 1929 Essay

Samuel Beckett's first published work, **"Dante... Bruno. Vico.. Joyce,"** appeared in the collection *Our Exagmination Round His Factification for Incamination of Work in Progress* (Paris: Shakespeare and Company, 1929).

- **Key Quote:** Beckett warns against rigid allegorical readings, stating famously: *"The danger is in the neatness of identifications."*
- Beckett emphasizes that Joyce uses Vico's cycles and Bruno's opposites dynamically; history is not a flat circle but an evolving, overlapping spiral.

*Further Scholarship:* Donald Phillip Verene's *Vico and Joyce* (State University of New York Press, 1987) provides the most comprehensive philosophical mapping of how Joyce integrates Vico's poetic wisdom and *verum-factum* into the dream-language of the novel.

### The Thunderclap in the Wake

Joyce mimics Vico's primal, civilization-starting thunder with ten 100-letter thunderwords (the last has 101 letters) scattered throughout the text. The most famous is the very first, representing the Fall of Finnegan and the waking of primitive man:

> "bababadalgharaghtakamminarronnkonnbronntonnerronntuonnthunntrovarrhounawnskawntoohoohoordenenthurnuk!" (FW 3.15-16)

This multi-lingual portmanteau of words for "thunder" acts as a Viconian terror-event that triggers the entire historical cycle of the book.

## 4. The Structural Mapping of the Four Books

Clive Hart's seminal study, *Structure and Motif in Finnegans Wake* (Northwestern UP, 1962), solidifies how the macro-structure of the text precisely maps to Vico's four parts.

### Table: Viconian Architecture of *Finnegans Wake*

| Book | Chapters | Page Range | Viconian Age | Thematic Characteristics | FW Focus |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Book I** | 8 | 3–216 | **Age of Gods** | Theocracy, genesis, myth, giants, origins | The Parents (HCE and ALP); the Fall; the rumors and history of the father |
| **Book II** | 4 | 219–399 | **Age of Heroes** | Aristocracy, conflict, games, rebellion | The Children (Shem, Shaun, Issy); the children taking over the parents' roles |
| **Book III** | 4 | 403–590 | **Age of Men** | Democracy, rationalism, dissolution, prose | Shaun's journey; the decline of the father into an old man; exhaustion of the cycle |
| **Book IV** | 1 | 593–628 | **Ricorso** | Return, awakening, dawn, the transition | The dawn; ALP's final monologue; the river returning to the sea, connecting to page 3 |

*Note: Within each Book, and even within individual chapters, the Viconian four-part cycle is repeated fractally, creating a "vicus of recirculation" (FW 3.02) across all scales of the text.*

## 5. Notes for SVG Graphic Generation

When translating this research into data visualizations (SVG graphics):

1. **The Circular Scaffold:** The overarching SVG should be circular or spiral to represent the *corso-ricorso*. Book IV (Ricorso) should serve as the transitional bridge connecting the end of Book III back to the beginning of Book I.
2. **Color Coding the Ages:**
   - *Book I (Gods):* Ethereal, thunder-focused, mythic colors (dark stormy purples or divine golds).
   - *Book II (Heroes):* Bold, clashing colors representing the Shem/Shaun rivalry (reds/blues).
   - *Book III (Men):* Faded, dusk-like or mundane tones reflecting the decline of the cycle.
   - *Book IV (Ricorso):* Dawn colors, water/river blues, reflecting ALP's flow back to the sea.
3. **Brunonian Overlays:** Include a diametric axis crossing through the center representing Shem and Shaun, demonstrating how they mirror each other at opposite points on the Viconian wheel (Coincidence of Opposites).
4. **Data Nodes:** Mark exact chapter divisions and the ten thunderwords as points on the circumference.
